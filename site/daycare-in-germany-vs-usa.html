"Free" Daycare in Germany? The Real Cost, State by State

Everyone says daycare in Germany is free. The truth is a lot more complicated, and where you live changes everything.

It's 7:45 on a Tuesday morning in Austin, Texas, and Sarah is standing in a hallway that smells like crayons and hand sanitizer, peeling her two-year-old off her leg while a director hands her a tablet to sign in. Before she leaves, she taps her card on a reader by the door: $420, payment for the next two weeks. She does this every other Friday, rain or shine, promotion or layoff, because without this building, she doesn't have a job.

At almost the same moment, six time zones away in Berlin, a father named Lukas walks his son through the door of a public daycare center, a Kita, down the street from their apartment. He signs a clipboard. He hangs up a backpack. Nobody swipes a card, nobody hands him an invoice. He'll pay €23 this month, and that's only because his son eats lunch there.

Somewhere between those two mornings is where most Americans get their idea of German childcare: free, generous, a signal that Germany simply solved the problem the US hasn't. There's truth buried in that. But "free childcare in Germany" is one of the most misleading phrases in the entire expat conversation, because whether Lukas's story or something much closer to Sarah's is your reality depends entirely on which of Germany's 16 states you happen to live in.

I'm Justin. I've lived in Germany for over 12 years, I've registered two kids for daycare here myself, and I'm not going to give you the tourist-brochure version. I'm going to walk you through what parents in every part of this country actually pay, state by state, the real numbers, not the headline.

The myth versus the patchwork

The myth is simple: Germany equals free daycare, full stop. The reality is a patchwork so uneven that two apartments 20 minutes apart, in two different states, can produce a monthly bill of zero euros in one and several hundred euros in the other, for two families that look identical on paper.

To be fair to the myth, it didn't come from nowhere. Compared to the US, where the national average for a single infant's spot at a daycare center runs somewhere around $1,230 a month, and can climb past $2,500 a month in states like Massachusetts or California, Germany's system really has pulled a huge number of families out of paying anything close to that. Several states have genuinely eliminated fees for large stretches of early childhood. That part of the myth is real. It's the "everywhere, for everyone, no matter what" part that falls apart the second you look at a map.

Land in one of these four states, and the thing you heard before moving here is, more or less, just true.

Move one category over and the promise gets an asterisk. "Free" in these states often really means free for part of the day, for part of childhood.

The narrow-window states

Free only right before school
North Rhine-WestphaliaFree only the final 2 years before school, ~age 4+
ThuringiaSame two-years-free model

Germany's most populous state, NRW, only waives fees for the final two years before a child starts school. Everything before that is full price, and in high-cost towns like Bergisch Gladbach, families with strong incomes have reported bills as high as €1,220 a month for a full-day spot for a child under four. If your kid is an infant or a toddler in either of these states, you're paying real money for a couple of years before the free window opens.

The real obstacle: finding a spot

Here's where it gets interesting, though, because the number on the invoice isn't actually the biggest obstacle German parents run into. It's finding a spot, a Kitaplatz, in the first place. Even in cities where care is completely free, waiting lists can stretch for a year or more, and some parents register with multiple facilities the moment they find out they're expecting, just to have a shot at a spot once parental leave runs out.

Price and access turned out to be two completely separate problems, and Germany solved the first one a lot more thoroughly than the second.

The math: Sarah vs. Lukas

Back in Austin, Sarah's $420 every two weeks adds up to a little over $10,000 a year, not the worst-case American number, just an ordinary one. Lukas, over in Berlin, will spend less on daycare this year than Sarah spends in a single month.

But if Lukas had been born 200 miles south, in Stuttgart, or in a Bavarian town that didn't qualify for the subsidy, his story could look a lot more like hers. The gap between them was never really America versus Germany. It was always which zip code.

What to actually do about it

  1. Look up your specific state, and just as important, your specific city or district. A state-level "it's free" headline can hide a municipality that still charges full price.

  2. Get on daycare waiting lists as early as legally possible, ideally the moment you know you're expecting, because availability, not cost, is the real bottleneck in most cities.

  3. Register for the monthly child benefit (Kindergeld) regardless of what state you're in. It's separate from daycare fees entirely, and every family qualifies.

  4. Even in the fully free states, budget for meals and optional extras. A small fee like Berlin's €23 is basically guaranteed no matter where you land.

Sarah's still going to tap her card every other Friday. Lukas is still going to walk his son past a hallway that never asks him for one. Both of those things are true, and so is everything in between them, depending on which of the 16 different versions of Germany you actually move to. That's the real price of "free" childcare here: it's not zero, it's just wildly, specifically local.
