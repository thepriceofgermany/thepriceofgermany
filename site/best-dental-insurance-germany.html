The Best Dental Insurance in Germany (and Why You Actually Need It)

German public health insurance pays for the check-up and then quietly steps back. A single implant can leave you 3,000 euros out of pocket. Here is how Zahnzusatzversicherung works, what the dentist actually charges, and the best dental insurance to buy as a foreigner.

The first time most Americans get German dental insurance wrong, it is at the dentist's office, holding a piece of paper called a Heil- und Kostenplan, doing mental math on a crown they assumed would be free because they have insurance. It is not free. It is 900 euros, and the public system is going to cover about a third of it.

This surprises people because the headline about Germany is always the same: great healthcare, nearly free, nobody goes bankrupt over a doctor. That is largely true for your body. It is much less true for your teeth. Dental is the one corner of German healthcare that works more like the American model than people expect, which means it is also the one corner where a cheap add-on policy quietly saves you thousands.

What German public insurance actually covers for your teeth

If you are on public health insurance (gesetzliche Krankenversicherung, or GKV), which is most people, your dental coverage is real but thin. It covers the basics and stops there.

Public insurance pays for your twice-yearly check-up, a share of a basic professional cleaning at some insurers, simple plastic or amalgam fillings, and standard treatment when something goes wrong. For dentures, crowns, and bridges it pays a Festzuschuss, a fixed subsidy tied to the cheapest standard solution, usually around 60 to 75 percent of that baseline if you have kept your Bonusheft stamped with yearly visits.

Here is the catch that costs people money: the subsidy is calculated on the cheapest acceptable fix, not the one you and your dentist actually want. The moment you choose a tooth-colored crown over a metal one, a ceramic inlay over a filling, or an implant over a gap, you are paying the difference. And the difference is where almost all the money is.

What it does not cover, and what that costs

These are the treatments the public system leaves mostly or entirely on you. The numbers are typical German private-pay prices, the kind that show up on that Heil- und Kostenplan before any insurance is applied.

Professional cleaning is the sneaky one. Most dentists here recommend it once or twice a year, most patients get it, and the public system covers little to none of it. Call it 100 to 240 euros a year that nobody warned you about. Everything below that line, the inlays, crowns, bridges, and implants, is where a bad year can cost more than your rent.

Germany vs the USA: closer than you would think

This is the part that breaks the usual story. On most healthcare, America is the expensive one. On teeth, the two systems rhyme, because in both countries dental is treated as a semi-separate thing you are expected to top up yourself.

In the US, dental is almost always a separate policy from your health insurance. A typical employer dental plan runs maybe 20 to 50 dollars a month, and it comes with an annual maximum that is frozen somewhere in 1970: often just 1,000 to 1,500 dollars a year. One implant and you have blown through the cap and you are paying the rest yourself, 3,000 to 5,000 dollars for a single tooth.

The German advantage is not that public insurance covers teeth better. It is that the fix, a supplementary dental policy, is cheap, uncapped in the good plans, and easy to buy. America bolts on a dental plan with a hard ceiling. Germany lets you bolt on one that, after a few years, can pay 90 to 100 percent of real costs with no annual maximum at all. It is the same pattern as personal liability insurance in Germany: a small annual premium that quietly removes a huge worst case.

How Zahnzusatzversicherung works

Zahnzusatzversicherung is a private supplementary policy that sits on top of your public insurance and pays the part the public system does not. A few things are worth understanding before you compare plans, because the monthly price is the least important number.

Reimbursement percentage

Each plan pays back a percentage of the treatment, often counting the public subsidy toward the total. A good comprehensive plan reaches 90 to 100 percent on dentures, crowns, and implants. Cheaper plans sit at 70 to 80 percent, which still turns a 3,000 euro implant into a few hundred.

Waiting periods and the ramp-up limit

This is the rule that trips people up. Most plans cap how much they pay in the first few years. You might be limited to a few hundred euros in year one, rising each year until the cap disappears entirely around year four or five. The policy is designed to be bought before you need it, not the week a crown breaks. There is usually also no coverage for treatment a dentist has already recommended or started before you signed up.

The Heil- und Kostenplan

Before any big treatment, your dentist writes a Heil- und Kostenplan, an itemized cost estimate. You send it to your insurer first and they tell you what they will pay. Skipping this step can mean skipping your reimbursement, so for anything expensive, get the plan and submit it before the drill comes out. Who actually needs it (and who can skip it)

Dental insurance is not automatic. Whether it pays off depends on your mouth and your main insurance.

You probably want it if you are on public insurance and you have a history of bad teeth, your family does, you grind, you already have crowns or fillings that may need redoing, or you simply want cleanings and future work covered without flinching at the estimate. For most people on GKV, it is the cheap-insurance-against-an-expensive-year math that makes sense.

You can probably skip it if you are on private health insurance (PKV). Private plans usually already include solid dental cover or let you upgrade it directly, so a separate dental policy would just be paying twice. Check your existing PKV dental terms before buying anything.

Buy dental insurance the year nothing is wrong. That is the only year you can.

Common questions about dental insurance in Germany

Does German public health insurance cover dental care?
Partly. Public insurance (GKV) covers the twice-yearly check-up, a share of a basic professional cleaning, simple fillings, and a fixed subsidy toward standard dentures. It does not cover implants, high-quality crowns, inlays, or most of the cost of good-looking restorations.
How much does dental insurance cost in Germany?
A supplementary dental policy (Zahnzusatzversicherung) typically starts around 9 to 11 euros a month for a basic plan and runs roughly 16 to 30 euros a month for a comprehensive one, depending on your age when you sign up.
Which is the best dental insurance in Germany for expats: Feather, Getsafe or Ottonova?
For most foreigners, Feather is the easiest pick: the quote, signup and claims are all in English, the entry price is low, and the comprehensive tier covers crowns, bridges and implants once the first-year limits pass. Is there a waiting period for German dental insurance?
Most modern dental policies have no formal waiting period, but they cap how much they pay in the first three to five years before the limit disappears. Treatment a dentist has already recommended or started before you sign up is not covered, so it pays to buy before you need it.
Do I need dental insurance if I have private health insurance?
Usually not. Private health insurance (PKV) often already includes strong dental cover or lets you upgrade it directly, so a separate dental policy would be paying twice. Check your existing PKV dental terms first.

What to do about it

If you are living in Germany on public insurance, here is the short version.

  1. Buy before you need it. The ramp-up limits mean a policy bought the week your tooth breaks pays you almost nothing. Sign up while your teeth are healthy and boring, you can start a Feather quote here →

  2. Compare the reimbursement percentage and the cover limits, not just the monthly price. A plan 4 euros more a month that pays 100 percent instead of 70 percent is not more expensive, it is cheaper the first time you need a crown.

  3. Keep your Bonusheft stamped. Going to your check-up every year raises the public subsidy on dentures later. It is free money for showing up.

  4. Always get the Heil- und Kostenplan and submit it first. For any expensive treatment, let the insurer confirm what they will pay before you start.

  5. If you are on private insurance, check what you already have. You may be covered, and a second dental policy would just double your premium for nothing.
