Why Nobody Riots Over the German Pension Bill

France rioted over a pension reform. Germany has the same problem and quietly pays. Here is where the money goes, who pays it, what other countries did, and what it means if you live and work here.

France rioted over a pension reform. Germany has the same problem and quietly pays. Here is where the money goes, who pays it, what other countries did, and what it means if you live and work here.

Remember when France burned over pensions? Barricades, strikes, tear gas, all because the government raised the retirement age by two years. Germany has the same problem, and in some ways a bigger one. The only difference is the symptoms. France riots. Germany quietly pays. I've lived in Germany for over twelve years, and this is where the money goes, who it comes from, and who ends up with the bill. Parts of this are my opinion, and I mark them as opinion.

Quick note: this is general information and my opinion. It is not tax, legal or financial advice, and I am not a financial adviser. Figures are rounded and change every year, so check current numbers before you decide anything.

The number that explains everything: 50.3%

The Staatsquote is the share of Germany's economy that runs through the government: 50.3 percent, measured for 2025. Roughly one out of every two euros spent in this country is spent by the state. In the United States the same measure is around 38 percent. A big number is not automatically bad. France is even higher, and the Nordic countries are high too. But those countries get something specific for it, and the real question is whether Germany does. Germany's constitution defines the country as a Sozialstaat, a social state: you hand over a large share of your income, and the state looks after you through life. Almost every benefit in that deal is paid for by the people who are working right now, not out of a pot of savings.

A German paycheck, split open

This is an illustration, not your payslip. Take a single employee who earns 4,000 euros gross per month. Brutto is gross, Netto is net. In between sit the social insurances.

Pension (Rentenversicherung)9.3% of gross, employer pays the same again
Health insurance (Krankenversicherung)around 8 to 9%, depends on the insurer
Unemployment insurance (Arbeitslosenversicherung)1.3%

Income tax comes on top, so a single earner at this level sees around a third of the gross wage disappear before the money arrives. Your employer pays a second share of the social contributions that never shows on your net pay, and put together, social contributions come to over 42 percent of a gross wage. Economists measure the gap between what an employer pays for a worker and what the worker takes home as the tax wedge. In the OECD's Taxing Wages report for 2025, Germany takes 49.3 percent of an average single worker's labor cost. That is the second highest in the developed world, behind only Belgium, and higher than France. The country with the burning barricades takes less from its workers than the country with the quiet commute.

How the pension works, and why it is running short

The German pension is a pay as you go system, the Umlageverfahren. Your contributions are not saved in an account with your name on it. They are paid out almost immediately to today's pensioners, and your own pension will be paid by whoever is working when you retire. It is a promise from one generation to the next, and it works beautifully while there are plenty of workers for every retiree. In the early 1960s there were about six contributors for every pensioner. Today it is a little over two, and through the 2030s the baby boomers finish retiring.

Germany did raise the retirement age, from 65 to 67, step by step, with the last step arriving in 2031. France's smaller reform set off the riots. So Germany already did the thing France rioted about; it just did it quietly, over nearly twenty years. In December 2025 the Bundestag then passed the Rentenpaket, which locks the pension level at 48 percent until 2031 and expands the Mütterrente, at a cost of about five billion euros a year. The federal audit office, the Bundesrechnungshof, estimates the package adds about 210 billion euros in extra costs through 2040. Both measures are popular, and both are paid for by people who are working today. In my opinion, the system only works while the base keeps growing, and Germany's base is shrinking.

The generation gap

In 2023 the median household under 35 had a net worth of about 17,000 euros. Households aged 55 to 64 had over 240,000, about fourteen times more. Young Germans also face the lowest homeownership rate in the European Union, about 47 percent, and the second highest tax burden in the developed world. At the same time, about one in five people over 65 is at risk of poverty, 19.5 percent in 2025 against 16.1 percent for the whole population. The system is expensive for the young, and it still leaves many retirees with little.

Why Germany doesn't riot

Part of it is history and a culture that prefers consensus: unions, employer federations and the government negotiate, called Sozialpartnerschaft, social partnership. Part of it is that the pain is spread out and invisible. It comes off your payslip before you ever see it, and nobody marches against a number they never held in their hands. But quiet does not mean satisfied. Millions work part time, many try to retire as early as possible, and at the ballot box the AfD has polled between roughly a quarter and 30 percent in recent surveys. That is a sign people are not as calm as the streets suggest.

To be fair, there is a real case for the German model: health insurance that covers almost everyone, unemployment insurance that turns a layoff into something less than a catastrophe, nearly free university and subsidized childcare. The debate is not security versus no security. It is who pays, how much, and for how long. And this is not only a German disease: the 2026 Social Security trustees report projects the US retirement fund can pay full benefits only until 2032. Every rich country with an ageing population has a version of it.

What other countries did

Sweden, in the 1990s, built a system with an automatic balancing mechanism that slows pension increases when the system runs short, and sends part of every contribution into an individual investment account. Denmark links its retirement age to life expectancy and confirmed it will reach 70 by 2040 for people born after 1970. The Netherlands is moving to individual pension pots, with existing schemes required to convert by 2028. Austria brought civil servants into the main system. None of it was painless, but it was decided before the crisis, not during it. Germany's own pension commission reported in June 2026 with 33 recommendations, including a Swedish style funded pension, and as I record this none of it is law.

What it means for you

Three general points, not advice. One: many economists expect the statutory pension to replace less of your income in the future than it does today. That is an expectation, not a certainty. Two: if you rely only on the Gesetzliche Rente, you are relying on a political promise, and many people add their own savings, such as a company pension (bAV), a private pension, or a simple portfolio in a Depot. Three: if you are an expat, check what happens to your contributions if you leave. For Americans, there is a social security agreement between the United States and Germany, so periods in each country can count toward each side. The details matter, so ask a qualified adviser.

Do this in ten minutes

  1. Open your last payslip (Gehaltsabrechnung). Find the lines for Rentenversicherung, Krankenversicherung, Arbeitslosenversicherung and Pflegeversicherung, and add them up.

  2. Find your Renteninformation, the letter from the Deutsche Rentenversicherung that usually arrives once a year. It shows what you have collected so far and what a pension might be.
