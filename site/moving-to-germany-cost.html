The plane ticket is the cheap part

Most people think the big cost of moving to Germany is the plane ticket and maybe a moving company. That's the myth. The reality is the plane ticket is almost the cheapest line item on the whole list. The real cost is buried in fees, deposits, and government requirements most people never hear about until they're already mid-process.

To be fair, the crowd that says moving here is cheap isn't wrong about everything. Compared to a lot of countries, Germany's actual bureaucratic fees are low, we're talking double-digit euros for a lot of the paperwork, not thousands. If you're single, flexible on housing, and shipping almost nothing, you genuinely can do this move light. That version of the move exists. It's just not the version most people actually end up living.

The visa and the blocked account

If you're coming from the US for work, you'll apply for a national visa at the German consulate, and the application fee itself is only €75. Cheap. But almost nobody qualifies on the visa fee alone. Depending on your visa type, you may also need to prove you can support yourself, which means opening a blocked account and depositing close to a thousand euros for every month you're not yet employed here. For a lot of people, that's €11,000 to €12,000 sitting frozen in a bank account before you've even booked a flight.

Health insurance isn't optional

Then there's health insurance, which in Germany isn't optional. You legally cannot register your address without proof of coverage. If your employer enrolls you in public health insurance, you're typically looking at somewhere around €280 to €500 a month depending on your income, split with your employer. If you're self-employed or between jobs, expect to pay that whole amount yourself, sometimes with private insurance running €300 to €500 a month or more.

Shipping a whole household

Now shipping, this is the one people budget worst for. If you're bringing real furniture and a full household, a shipping container from the US runs somewhere between $6,000 and $13,000 door to door, depending on container size and how far inland you're going on both ends.

If that number makes you wince, you're not alone. It's why most experienced expats will tell you to sell almost everything, start over, and just ship boxes instead. A partial container, sharing space with other people's shipments, can bring that down to somewhere in the low thousands, but it's still real money, and it still takes three to six weeks to arrive.

Rent deposits and agent fees

Renting is where the deposits hit. German landlords are legally allowed to ask for up to three months of cold rent as a security deposit, cold rent meaning before utilities. On an average city apartment renting for say €1,200 a month, that's up to €3,600 just sitting with your landlord, on top of your first month's rent. Add a real estate agent's commission, which can run close to two months' rent in competitive cities, and you can be €6,000 to €7,000 deep before you've unpacked a single box.

Germany's hidden second layer of costs

Here's where it gets interesting. Even after the visa, the insurance, the shipping, and the deposit, you're still not actually done, because Germany has an entire second layer of setup costs that catch almost everyone off guard.

You'll register your address at the local citizen's office within two weeks of moving in, which is free, but it unlocks a chain of paid next steps: your residence permit card, typically €50 to €150, and a mandatory household broadcasting fee of about €18 a month, whether you own a TV or not.

And then there's the very unglamorous cost of just furnishing an empty apartment, because a lot of German rentals come completely bare: no kitchen, no light fixtures, sometimes not even a kitchen sink. Buying a basic kitchen alone can run anywhere from €2,000 to €8,000 if you can't inherit one from the last tenant.

Dana's real total

So let's go back to Dana. Her $8,000 budget covered the flight, the first month's rent, and basically nothing else. What actually hit her account over six weeks was closer to $19,000: visa and blocked account funds, two months of private health insurance before her job coverage kicked in, a partial shipping container, the deposit and agent fee on her apartment, and a secondhand kitchen she had to buy from the outgoing tenant because the unit was completely empty.

None of that was a scam or bad luck. It's just what the real total looks like when nobody adds up the line items in advance.

What I'd actually do differently

Here's what I'd tell a friend making this move.

  1. Budget $15,000 to $20,000 total if you're moving a household, not just yourself in a suitcase, and treat anything lower as the light version of this move, not the default.

  2. Open your blocked account and start the visa paperwork the moment you have a job offer, because that clock is the longest one in the whole process.

  3. Ship less than you think you need to. Furniture is almost always cheaper to rebuy in Germany than to ship.

  4. Budget for the invisible costs specifically: the kitchen, the broadcasting fee, the agent commission. Those are the ones that blow up an otherwise careful budget.
