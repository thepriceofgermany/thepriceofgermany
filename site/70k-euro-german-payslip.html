Inside a 70k Euro German Payslip: Where Your Salary Goes

A 70,000 euro salary in Germany lands as about 3,575 euros a month. Here is the payslip line by line, what the deductions buy you, how it compares with the same salary in the US, and what is left after rent.

Imagine you get a job offer in Germany for 70,000 euros a year. That is about 78,000 dollars. It sounds great. But here is what lands in your bank account every month: about 3,575 euros.

I've lived in Germany for over twelve years. In the video I show where the other 2,260 euros go, line by line, and what you actually get for it. This post has the same payslip in written form, so you can keep the numbers.

Quick note: this is general information and an example, not tax or financial advice, and I am not a tax adviser. The example is a single person, tax class 1, no children, no church tax, using the rules for 2026 and my own calculation. Your payslip will be different, so use these numbers as a map, not as a promise.

Building the payslip

The gross salary is 70,000 euros a year, which is 5,833 euros a month. The employer pays more than that. On top of your gross salary, it pays its own half of the social insurance, about 14,800 euros a year, so this job costs the company about 84,800 euros before a few small extra levies. You never see that money.

Your gross salary70,000 EUR
Employer half of social insuranceabout 14,800 EUR
Cost to the companyabout 84,800 EUR

Line by line: social insurance

Four lines come off your gross salary before the tax office gets anything.

Pension insurance (Rentenversicherung). The rate is 18.6 percent, split in half. Your half is 9.3 percent, which is 542.50 euros a month.

Unemployment insurance (Arbeitslosenversicherung). Your half is 1.3 percent, about 76 euros a month.

Health insurance (Krankenversicherung). The base rate is 14.6 percent, again split in half, so 7.3 percent for you. On top comes an extra charge, the Zusatzbeitrag, that depends on your health fund. The official average is 2.9 percent and you pay half of it, 1.45 percent, so together it is 8.75 percent. There is a ceiling: only the first 69,750 euros of salary are charged. That makes about 509 euros a month.

Long term care insurance (Pflegeversicherung). The total rate is 3.6 percent. If you are over 23 and have no children, you pay an extra 0.6 percent on your side, so 2.4 percent in most states. That is about 139.50 euros a month.

That is about 15,200 euros a year.

Income tax

Now the income tax, the Lohnsteuer. It is not charged on your full gross salary. First the tax office subtracts allowances: the standard work expense allowance of 1,230 euros, a small allowance for special expenses of 36 euros, and most of your pension, health and care contributions, which are deductible too. That leaves a taxable income of about 53,800 euros.

On that amount the 2026 tax formula gives about 11,900 euros a year. That is about 992 euros a month, and an average rate of 17 percent of your gross salary.

The solidarity surcharge, the Soli, is zero here, because it only starts when your tax is above 20,350 euros. And the top rate of 42 percent only starts at about 69,900 euros of taxable income, so you are nowhere near it.

The result

Gross5,833 EUR

That is about 61 percent of the gross salary. About 39 percent never reaches you.

The first time you see this, it hurts. But notice what is not on this list: a separate health insurance bill. And your employer pays a second share on top. Whether this is a good deal is a real debate. What I can say for sure is that you should know these numbers before you accept an offer.

What the deductions buy

  • Health. Statutory health insurance covers your doctor visits and hospital care.
  • If you lose your job. Unemployment benefit pays about 60 percent of your last net salary, if you have no children.
  • If you are sick. Your employer keeps paying your salary for up to six weeks. After that, the health fund pays sick pay, usually 70 percent of your gross salary, capped at 90 percent of your net.
  • Pension. The pension line builds a public pension claim. Whether that will be enough is a separate debate, and I covered it in my report about the German pension.

The same salary in the US

Now the comparison Americans always ask for. At an exchange rate of about 1.12, 70,000 euros is about 78,400 dollars. For a single filer under the 2026 rules, with no state tax yet and the standard deduction of 16,100 dollars, federal income tax comes to about 8,400 dollars. Social Security and Medicare take 7.65 percent, about 6,000 dollars. That leaves about 64,000 dollars a year, or about 5,330 dollars a month.

Grossabout 78,400 USD

Social Security + Medicare (7.65%)about 6,000 USD
NET per yearabout 64,000 USD

The German net is 3,575 euros, about 4,000 dollars a month. So on paper, the American keeps roughly 1,300 dollars more each month.

But the American number is not finished yet. State income tax comes off, if your state has one. The health insurance premium comes off: according to a 2025 employer survey (KFF), it averages about 1,400 dollars a year for an employee with single coverage, plus an average deductible of about 1,900 dollars before most care is covered. And nothing is being saved for retirement in that number yet.

My take: the American number is bigger, and in a good year that is nice. The German number is smaller, but more of the risk is already paid for: sick pay, unemployment benefit, health care, a public pension. I am not saying one system wins. You cannot compare a net salary without asking what you still have to buy. I went deeper on this in the salary report.

What is left after rent

What is left depends mostly on one thing: your rent. Say a 55 square meter apartment. Asking rents on a big portal averaged around 9 euros per square meter nationwide in early 2026 (ImmoScout24), about 500 euros cold rent. In the eight biggest cities, new leases average closer to 17.5 euros per square meter (JLL, first half of 2026), about 965 euros.

What changes your number

  • Tax class. If you are married, the tax class changes how much is withheld each month, and the final bill is settled in your tax return.
  • Church tax. It adds about 8 or 9 percent on top of your income tax, which here would be around 950 to 1,070 euros a year. Check your own rate.
  • Children. Having children removes the extra care insurance charge.
  • A company pension or a private health plan changes the picture again.

This is a single person with no car and no kids, so add those and the numbers move fast.

A note for Americans

You file a US tax return every year, even when you live in Germany. At this kind of income, credits for the German tax you paid often reduce the US bill a lot, but that depends on your case, so ask a cross border tax professional. I covered the accounts that cause the most trouble in my video about the five tax accounts expats keep missing.

Before you accept an offer

  1. Ask for the gross salary and the monthly net, or run the number through the official government calculator.

  2. Check the extra charge of your health fund (Zusatzbeitrag). It differs from fund to fund, and you can switch.

  3. Check your tax class.

  4. File a tax return. The work expense allowance is only 1,230 euros, and costs above it can come back to you.
