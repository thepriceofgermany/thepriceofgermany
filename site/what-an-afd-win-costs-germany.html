What an AfD Win Really Costs Germany

It's early morning in Halle, in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, and Torsten is standing at his kitchen counter with a coffee going cold, staring at an energy bill that's 20% higher than it was two winters ago. He runs a small auto parts shop three blocks away, and the last few years have felt like running in place. Costs up, customers cautious, and a federal government in Berlin that, from where he's standing, hasn't done much about either. This September, his state votes for a new government, and for the first time in his life the polls show one party within striking distance of an outright majority: Alternative for Germany, or the AfD.

Across town, an American named Andrea is opening a very different kind of email. She moved to Halle eight years ago after marrying a German, and now runs a small consulting business connecting local manufacturers with buyers in the US. One of her longest clients just wrote to say they're pausing a supply contract. Not because the numbers changed, but because their board wants to wait and see what happens with the election before committing more money to the region.

Same city, same election, two completely different reactions. One voting out of frustration, one watching her business absorb the consequences of other people's uncertainty before a single vote is even cast. So what's actually true here? What can a state election in Germany really put in motion, and what does it cost the people voting and the people just trying to live and work there?

I'm Justin, and I've lived in Germany for over 12 years. This is The Price of Germany, where we break down the real numbers behind life here, the costs nobody puts on a postcard. This time the cost isn't a grocery bill, it's a political one, and we're walking through it with a level head, not a hot take.

The myth: new currency, closed borders

Here's the myth going around, on both sides: that a state election like this one is basically symbolic, or that if the AfD wins big this fall, Germany wakes up the next day with a different currency and closed borders. Neither is true. What actually changes, and what doesn't, comes down to a detail most coverage skips entirely: what a state government in Germany can and can't actually do.

Why the AfD is polling above 40 percent

Before we get into that, it's worth being honest about why the AfD is polling where it is, above 40% in Saxony-Anhalt in several 2026 polls, within reach of a majority on its own. This isn't happening in a vacuum. The former East German states have real grievances: wages that still lag the West more than three decades after reunification, an industrial base battered by years of high energy costs, and a sense, not unfounded, that Berlin's mainstream parties show up every four years and then disappear.

Immigration is a genuine flashpoint too, not just online noise. A lot of people voting AfD aren't voting for a manifesto line by line, they're voting against feeling ignored. Pretending it's all fringe doesn't explain 40% of anything.

What's actually on the ballot

So here's what's actually on the ballot. Saxony-Anhalt votes this September alongside Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, while Baden-Württemberg in the West already went to the polls back in March. In Saxony-Anhalt specifically, the AfD isn't just leading. Multiple 2026 polls put the party in the low to mid 40s, which would make it the first German state where the AfD could plausibly govern outright rather than sit as the largest opposition bloc.

What a state government really controls

If that happens, a state government gets to do more than people assume. States run the police, control school curriculum and university funding, oversee cultural and media institutions, and staff a large share of the regional civil service. A state's governor also gets a vote in the Bundesrat, the federal council, which has to sign off on much national legislation. That's real, day-to-day authority, not decoration.

Here's what gets lost in the headlines, though. A state government cannot, on its own, pull Germany out of the EU, drop the euro, or rewrite national immigration law. Those are federal and EU-level powers, locked into the constitution, and changing them would take control of the national parliament, almost certainly a coalition partner, and in some cases negotiation with 26 other EU countries. Saxony-Anhalt's election by itself doesn't touch any of that directly.

What the AfD wants nationally (Dexit)

Still, it's worth knowing what the AfD says it wants if it ever holds that level of power, because that trajectory is part of what this vote is really about. Nationally, the party has called for deep tax cuts, scrapping the wealth and inheritance tax, tightening asylum law well past what current German courts allow, and, most dramatically, a negotiated exit from the EU, sometimes called Dexit, including dropping the euro for a revived Deutsche Mark.

The real price tag: leaving the euro

That last one is where the real price tag shows up. Independent economists, not campaign staffers, have run the numbers on a euro exit. Berenberg Bank's chief economist estimates it could cost Germany more than 10% of its economic output over a decade. An economist at ING called it, bluntly, the kind of shock that could trigger a full-blown financial crisis. Researchers at the DIW Berlin institute have warned it would put millions of jobs at risk. These are projections, not facts on the ground, and estimates vary. But three independent institutions landing in the same neighborhood is not nothing.

The Brexit comparison

For a real-world comparison instead of a hypothetical, look at Brexit. The UK's own independent budget office estimated that leaving the EU would shrink long-run output by roughly 4%, and trade with Europe has measurably dropped since. That's a country that only left the trade bloc, not the currency. Leaving the euro, far more tightly wired into Germany's economy, would be a bigger structural change than what Britain went through.

The cost already being paid

Here's where it gets interesting, and it's what actually happened to Andrea's contract. Investors and researchers don't wait for a policy to pass before reacting to it. They price in the risk the moment it becomes plausible. That's happening in parts of Eastern Germany right now: investors and partner companies quietly slowing deals. Not because Dexit happened, but because it's now a real enough possibility to show up in a risk assessment. The cost isn't waiting for election night. For some businesses, it's already being paid.

Back to Halle: real vs myth

So back to Halle. Torsten's vote this September genuinely does control who runs the police, the schools, and a chunk of the civil service where he lives, and it hands his state's governor a seat at the federal table. That part is real and immediate. What it doesn't do, by itself, is bring back the Deutsche Mark or shut the border overnight.

Andrea's situation is different, and in some ways more immediate. Her client isn't reacting to a law that passed, they're reacting to uncertainty itself, and that bill is already on the table no matter how September turns out.

How to read any election: 4 checks

  1. Figure out exactly what office is on your ballot and what it legally controls. A state election and a federal election aren't the same vote with different math.

  2. Read the party's actual platform, not the clip making the rounds online. The headline version and the manifesto are rarely the same thing.

  3. If your money or business touches Germany, watch the federal and EU-level developments that would actually move markets. Not every regional poll.

  4. When you see a dramatic economic figure, check whether it's from one source or several. Several independent institutions landing in the same range, the way the Dexit estimates do, is a very different signal from a single number.

The takeaway

Torsten still hasn't decided how he's voting, and honestly, that's fine. It's his call to make with real information instead of slogans. Andrea, meanwhile, is already rewriting her quarterly forecast, because sometimes the most expensive thing on a German ballot isn't even what wins. Whatever happens this September, that's the kind of cost that never makes it onto a postcard.
