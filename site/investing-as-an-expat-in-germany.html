Why Investing Is So Hard for Expats

When you move to Germany, you expect the paperwork and the language to be hard. Nobody warns you investing is too: blocked brokers, two tax systems, and PFIC traps for Americans. Here is why, and what you can actually do about it.

When you move to Germany, you expect the paperwork and the language to be hard. Nobody warns you investing is too: blocked brokers, two tax systems, and PFIC traps for Americans. Here is why, and what you can actually do about it.

When you move to Germany, you expect the hard parts to be the paperwork, the language, and the apartment. Nobody warns you that investing is hard too. The apps ask for things you don't have yet. Bank advisers push products you don't understand. And if your home country has its own rules, they can follow you here. I've lived in Germany for over twelve years, and this is why investing feels so hard for expats, where that comes from, and what you can actually do. Americans, there's a section just for you near the end.

Quick note: I'm not a tax adviser or a financial adviser, and this is general information, not advice. Rules and broker policies change, and your situation is your own. Before you move real money, talk to a tax adviser who knows German rules, and if your home country taxes you, those rules too.

Why most Germans don't invest

Start with the surprise. In 2025, the German Share Institute (Deutsches Aktieninstitut) counted 14.1 million people who owned shares, stock funds, or ETFs, about one in five people aged 14 and over. It's a record, up 2 million in a single year, and most of them hold funds or ETFs, not single stocks. The good news is that Germany is waking up. The bad news is that for decades the country sat near the bottom of the rich-world list. That matters for you: your German coworkers, your Steuerberater, and your bank adviser often grew up in a culture where owning stocks was seen as gambling, so when you ask basic questions, don't expect clear answers.

The history: inflation, pensions, Telekom

There's no single reason, it's a stack of shocks. First, the memory of lost savings: in 1923, hyperinflation destroyed the value of the German mark, and a 1948 currency reform cut savings again. The lesson passed down was that money in assets can vanish, so stay close to things you can touch. Second, the state promised to take care of you: Bismarck introduced old-age insurance in 1889, and in 1957 West Germany switched to a pay-as-you-go system where today's workers fund today's pensioners. Why build a portfolio when the pension is a promise?

Third, the Telekom shock. In November 1996, Deutsche Telekom went public, marketed as the people's share, the Volksaktie. About 1.9 million private investors bought in, roughly 650,000 of them for the first time. The price peaked near €103 in March 2000, then fell far below the issue price, and a whole generation of first-time investors felt burned. Fourth, the Neuer Markt, Germany's stock market for young tech companies, collapsed in the dot-com bust. Add the love of the Sparbuch (savings book) and the Bausparvertrag (building-savings contract), a preference for a bank adviser over an app, and a belief that real estate is the safe path, and investing felt foreign to a lot of people. The change is real now (Germans aged 14 to 39 are the largest group of share owners), but the system around them was built for people who do it the German way, in German accounts, in euros, as German tax residents. If you're not that person, you'll keep hitting walls.

German investment tax, in plain English

The German side is simpler than the US system. A flat tax, the Abgeltungsteuer, is usually 25 percent on investment gains, plus the solidarity surcharge, which brings it to 26.375 percent, with church tax on top if you pay it. Your German broker usually withholds it automatically. Before that, every adult gets an allowance, the Sparer-Pauschbetrag, of €1,000 of investment income a year (€2,000 for married couples filing together). To use it you give your broker a Freistellungsauftrag, an exemption order; forget it and you pay tax you didn't owe.

Two fund details catch people out. For many stock funds, 30 percent of the gains are tax-free, the Teilfreistellung. And accumulating ETFs, the ones that don't pay out dividends, are taxed on a yearly estimate called the Vorabpauschale, based on a base rate set each year (3.20 percent for 2026), and it's due even if you sold nothing. An illustration, not a forecast: on €50,000 in an accumulating stock ETF, the Vorabpauschale is €50,000 times 3.20 percent times 0.7, which is €1,120; after the 30 percent partial exemption, €784 is taxable; at 26.375 percent that's about €207 of tax before your allowance, and if your Freistellungsauftrag covers it you may pay nothing. The real figure is also capped by the actual gain for the year. Honestly, the German side isn't bad: a flat tax, an allowance, and your broker handles the rest. The hard part is everything around it.

The 7 expat hurdles

These come from being an expat, whatever your passport says, and notice that none of them is about picking the right fund. They're about access, paperwork, and rules from two countries.

The seven expat hurdles
1. PaperworkGerman address, tax ID (Steuer-ID), German bank account
2. Your home countryForeign-account reporting, maybe citizenship-based tax
3. Two tax systemsA treaty decides who taxes what; withholding at source
4. The productsGerman brokers sell UCITS funds; your home fund may not exist here
5. The adviceCommission-driven; ask for a fee-only Honorarberater
6. MovingAn address change can restrict or close your account
7. Language and pensionsGerman contracts; portability depends on social-security deals

A few of these deserve a line. Your Steuer-ID letter can take a few weeks after you arrive, so start early. A tax treaty between Germany and your home country usually decides what happens when both want a piece, and dividends from foreign companies can have tax withheld at source that a treaty may reduce, so keep your statements for two returns. Most retail funds here are UCITS funds with the standard Key Information Document, so German brokers can sell them; the exact fund you used back home often isn't offered, though a similar European fund usually is. And tell your broker before you move, not after, because an address change can get an account restricted in either direction.

How to invest anyway

This is options, not advice, and the rule is: choose a simple plan you can keep for decades, and fix the paperwork first. For most people in Germany with a German-app broker, that looks like a broad, low-cost world ETF bought on a monthly savings plan, with the Freistellungsauftrag filed, and an emergency fund kept in a Tagesgeld account first so you never have to sell in a panic. Ask any broker five questions and write down the answers: Do you accept clients with my nationality and German address? Which funds can I buy? Do you provide tax reports I can also use for my home country? What are all the fees? And what happens to my account if I move?

For the tax-advantaged accounts (the company pension, the Basisrente, and the new Altersvorsorgedepot that starts on January 1, 2027 as the successor to Riester), I went through all five in detail in the German retirement accounts guide, and the broader saving-vs-investing culture in why Germans don't invest. The one thing I'd tell a friend: don't let the complexity make you do nothing. Sort out access, learn the two or three rules that apply to you, spend an hour with a specialist, and start small. A boring plan you keep beats a clever one you never begin.

For Americans: FATCA, the KID rule, and the PFIC

If you're a US citizen, green-card holder, or someone who passes the substantial-presence test, you have three traps on top of everything above. (Not American? You can skip this, though it's a good look at what a home country with its own rules can mean.)

Trap one is FATCA. This US law pushes foreign banks to report accounts held by US persons to the IRS. It's a lot of work and risk, so many foreign firms simply decline American customers. Trade Republic, for example, says on its own support page that US persons can't open an account, and other German brokers have closed or refused American accounts too. The list changes, so ask each one. Trap two is the KID rule. Under EU rules called PRIIPs, a broker can't sell certain products to retail clients unless the issuer provides a standard Key Information Document, and most US-listed ETFs don't have one, so many European brokers block them for everyone in the EU, including Americans who'd love to buy their old familiar fund. Owning one isn't illegal; buying more is often blocked. Trap three is the PFIC (passive foreign investment company). Most European ETFs and funds count as PFICs for US tax, which can mean filing Form 8621 for each fund every year, and without an election the default rules can tax gains at the highest ordinary rate plus an interest charge, instead of the lower capital-gains rate.

Two elections can soften the PFIC hit: a QEF election taxes you yearly on your share of the fund's income, but only works if the fund gives the right annual statement (few European funds do), and a mark-to-market election taxes gains and losses each year on the year-end value for funds that qualify as marketable stock. Both come with paperwork, deadlines, and costs, and a late election can be a problem, so this is where you want a specialist. PFIC rules are real, but they're not a reason to panic or hide anything, they're a reason to choose investments with the rules in mind from day one instead of finding out three years later.

What Americans usually look at (options, not advice, each needing a check with your own adviser): a broker that accepts US persons living abroad (Interactive Brokers is the name that comes up most in expat circles, not a recommendation, policies change); funds that aren't PFICs, like a US-domiciled index fund, which avoids the problem entirely but which a German broker often can't sell you because of the KID rule, which is why the broker choice and the fund choice go together; a real plan for any European fund you already hold; and keeping an existing US retirement account such as an IRA or 401k, whose German treatment can be complicated, so don't move or add money without a cross-border adviser. The short version: the broker may refuse you, the fund may be blocked, and the fund that's allowed may be taxed as a penalty, so plan the order: broker first, product second, paperwork third.

Mistakes to avoid, and what to do this week

The common mistakes: buying a fund without checking how your home country taxes it; forgetting the Freistellungsauftrag; signing a high-cost insurance-wrapped product you don't understand; not telling your broker you moved; and waiting years to start because it feels complicated. For Americans, add two: buying a European ETF without checking PFIC status, and forgetting the FBAR. Time in the market matters more than the perfect setup.

  1. List every account you have, in Germany and back home, and note the balances. You'll need this for both tax systems anyway.

  2. Ask your broker whether they accept you (nationality plus German address), and which funds you can actually buy.

  3. Book one hour with an adviser who knows both countries, especially before you touch a US retirement account or a European fund as an American.

  4. Pick one simple plan, file your Freistellungsauftrag, and automate it. Solve access first, then start small.
