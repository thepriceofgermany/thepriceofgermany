Techno Was Born in Detroit. Now Berlin's Clubs Are Losing Money

Germany did not invent techno. Detroit did. But Germany is where techno became a culture, an industry and now a problem. Here is the story from Detroit to Berlin, what Germany makes from it today, and what a 2026 study says about the clubs, with an interview with Kevin Saunderson.

Germany did not invent techno. Detroit did. But Germany is where techno became a culture, an industry, and now, a problem. This year, a study of Berlin clubs found that almost four in ten are losing money.

I've lived in Germany for over twelve years. When the Detroit Lions came to play in Munich, I sat down with Kevin Saunderson, one of the three men who started techno. In the video I tell how a sound from Detroit built a German scene, what that scene makes today, and why some of the places that carry it are struggling. This post has the same story in written form, so you can keep the numbers.

Quick note: the club figures below are survey results, not a count of every club. Juan Atkins is often credited as the originator, together with Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson. What Kevin says in the interview is his own account, and I mark it as that. The part called My take is my opinion.

Born in Detroit

Start in Detroit, in the early nineteen eighties. Three friends from the suburb of Belleville: Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson. History calls them the Belleville Three.

Atkins was the first of them to make records. He formed a duo called Cybotron. He was reading Alvin Toffler's book The Third Wave, and he loved the idea of people who master technology instead of fearing it. The sound was synthesizers and drum machines, with a clear echo of the German band Kraftwerk.

Atkins used the word techno for it. In 1985, as Model 500, he released No UFO's, a record often credited with carrying the Detroit sound overseas. Then in 1988, a British compilation put the word on the cover: Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit. That is why Atkins is so often called the originator.

Who invented techno? Kevin's answer

I asked Kevin directly: who invented techno, the Germans or Juan Atkins? His answer was short. "No doubt Juan Atkins."

His argument, in his own words: Kraftwerk created the keys of electronic music, and they used early advanced technology. But their vision was more like a band performing, it was not made for the dance floor, and it did not have a brand. Juan had a vision as a futurist from the start, he gave the sound a name, and Kevin says you will not see that name on a Kraftwerk record until after Juan's records came out. That is his account, not settled history.

A word on that name. Juan Atkins died on October 31, 2026, at the age of 64, just before a set in Mexico City. As of the recording, a cause has not been confirmed. Whatever people argue about, his is the name almost everyone starts with.

From Detroit to Berlin

Now notice the loop. A German band inspired Detroit, and now Detroit was about to inspire Germany. In 1989, the Berlin Wall fell. Berlin was suddenly full of empty buildings, and almost no rules for using them. That summer, a Berlin DJ called Dr. Motte started a small parade for peace, love and music. The first Love Parade drew about 150 people.

Kevin remembers that time from the road. When the Wall came down, he first came over to tour Germany with his group Inner City, a 30 day bus tour with Kool and the Gang and the SOS Band. He puts it at roughly 1989 or 1990. On stage he held up a piece of the Wall. His words: it was a special moment.

Tresor and the Berlin scene

A promoter named Dimitri Hegemann had already opened a club called UFO in 1988, playing early acid house. In March 1991, he opened Tresor, in an old bank vault, close to where the Wall had run. The first release on his label, Tresor Records, came from Underground Resistance, a Detroit collective. DJs from Detroit, like Jeff Mills, played there in the early years.

Detroit supplied the sound. Berlin supplied the empty space, and a crowd that wanted something new.

Why did the sound land so strongly in Germany? Kevin points to timing: the Wall came down just as the music was coming out of Detroit, the British compilation spread the word, and then Underground Resistance brought a tougher, more industrial sound that, in his words, really popped off.

The Love Parade

By the late nineties, the Love Parade was the biggest techno event on earth. Its peak is often given as one and a half million people, in 1999. In July 2010, in Duisburg, a crowd crush killed 21 people. The organizers ended the Love Parade for good. A new version came back in 2022 as Rave the Planet, run by Dr. Motte's team as a party and a demonstration.

What Germany makes from techno

So what does Germany make from all of this? Four things, and then the clubs themselves.

ToolsAbleton: Berlin 1999, Live 2001
Touristsabout 1.5 billion EUR a year (2018)
FestivalsParookaville: about 75,000 a day

Two notes. The heritage listing is a national list, not a UNESCO World Heritage Site, even though people often describe it that way. And Native Instruments, the Berlin company behind Traktor and Maschine, filed for preliminary insolvency in January 2026.

And then the clubs themselves: the places where DJs, bookers, security and bartenders actually earn a living. So techno is a cultural treasure, a software industry and a tourism magnet. You would expect the clubs to be thriving. Many of them are not.

Why the clubs are squeezed

In August 2026, Berlin's Clubcommission presented a new study, commissioned by the city's economy department. The survey ran in March and April. In 2025, 61 percent of clubs covered their costs. In 2017, it was 79 percent. So 39 percent ended the year with a loss.

The dance floors are not empty: 83 percent of clubs say they are at least half full. The problem is the business model.

Seventy three percent of clubs say guests are drinking less alcohol, and 57 percent say people stay for a shorter time. And the costs: 64 percent name staff, 62 percent name running costs, 60 percent name guests with less money to spend, and 54 percent name rent.

Since 2020, at least 24 clubs and venues have closed. About 25 new ones have opened. So this is not a collapse. It is a squeeze.

It is not only Berlin

In April 2025, a monitoring by the live music association LiveKomm covered 245 venues and festivals across Germany. About a third said their existence was under threat, and 78 percent said they needed public funding.

And in October 2026, Tresor.West in Dortmund, the sister club of the famous Berlin Tresor, announced it will close at the end of this year. Its founder, Dimitri Hegemann, named falling visitor numbers, rising costs and changing nightlife habits. He said a techno only program no longer pays in Dortmund.

My take, and it is my opinion: Germany put techno on its cultural heritage list, and then left the clubs to compete with festivals, rising wages and a generation that drinks less. A heritage listing is a title. It does not pay the rent. If a country calls something heritage, I think it should look after the buildings and the people who run them.

The whole loop

German machines inspired Detroit. Detroit invented techno. Berlin gave it a home and a crowd, and now Germany has to decide whether it wants to keep the places where it lives.

I asked Kevin whether he ever expected European culture to take to the music so strongly. He said he knew early that it was meant for everybody, and that the vision from the start was for it to be for the world. He did not know it would grow like a virus, as he put it, and still be going 40 something years later. His label, KMS Records, has its 40th anniversary in 2027, and he is planning showcases around the world.

Thank you to Kevin Saunderson for his time. Tell me in the comments on the video: what is the best club night you have had in Germany?
