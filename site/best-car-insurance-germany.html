The Best Car Insurance in Germany for Expats (AXA vs Feather vs DEVK)

Car insurance in Germany is mandatory, and that is where the trap starts

The second you register a car in Germany you are legally required to carry Kfz-Haftpflicht, third-party liability. Driving without it is not a fine, it is a criminal offense. That is the floor. On top of it sit two optional layers: Teilkasko (partial, which covers theft, glass, storm, fire, and animal collisions) and Vollkasko (comprehensive, which also pays for damage to your own car, including when the crash is your fault).

Liability only is cheap. The averages float around 250 to 300 euros a year. Add Teilkasko and you are usually somewhere in the 350 to 600 range. Full Vollkasko, which is what the bank will insist on if you financed or leased the car, commonly runs 700 to 1,200 euros a year or more. The exact number depends on your car, your postal code, your age, and above all your no-claims class, which we will get to, because that is the number that quietly doubles an expat's quote.

The SF system: why new arrivals get quoted like teenagers

Here is the mechanism almost nobody explains before you move. German car insurance is priced on your Schadenfreiheitsklasse (SF), your no-claims class. Every year you drive without an at-fault claim bumps you up a class, and a higher class means a bigger discount. A long-time German driver might sit at SF20 or higher and pay a small fraction of the base rate.

The problem for a foreigner: if the insurer starts you at SF0, as if you had never driven, you pay the full, un-discounted rate, often two to three times what a local with the same car pays. That is the single biggest reason expats overpay. The fix is to bring your no-claims history with you. If you were insured in the EU, the UK, Switzerland, Ukraine or Bosnia and Herzegovina, that record can usually transfer. And some insurers will start eligible newcomers at SF3, roughly three years of credit, without proof, which works out to about 48 percent off on average.

You will also hear about the eVB number, a 7-character electronic confirmation of insurance. The registration office, the Zulassungsstelle, will not hand you plates without it. The good news is you get it the moment you accept an offer, so insurance and registration can happen the same day.

Germany vs the USA: the rare win for the German side

This is one of the few bills that is clearly lower in Germany, and it is not close. In the US, full-coverage car insurance now averages somewhere around 2,300 to 2,600 dollars a year, and in plenty of states it runs well above that. The honest counterpoint: American policies usually bundle in higher liability limits and some medical coverage, and many Americans drive bigger cars and more miles. Which cover level do you actually need?

Liability only (Haftpflicht) is the legal minimum and the right call for an older car you own outright and could replace without pain. It protects everyone else from you, but nothing of yours.

Teilkasko makes sense if your car would hurt to lose to theft, a storm, a fire, or a deer on a country road, which in much of Germany is a real risk. Vollkasko is for newer, financed, or leased cars, and the leasing company will usually require it. A rough rule: new or under roughly five years old, go Vollkasko; paid-off and middle-aged, Teilkasko; old runabout, liability only.

The cheapest German policy is not cheap if it costs you a weekend with a dictionary and a botched claim. For an expat, English is a feature you are allowed to pay for.

Is car insurance mandatory in Germany?
Yes. At a minimum you must carry Kfz-Haftpflicht, third-party liability, and driving without it is a criminal offense, not just a fine. Teilkasko (partial) and Vollkasko (comprehensive) are optional add-ons that also cover damage to your own car.

What is an eVB number and when do I need it?
