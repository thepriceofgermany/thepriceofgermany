Can You Actually Survive on Minimum Wage in Germany?

It's the last Friday of the month in Dortmund, and Jana is wiping down the glass counter of a neighborhood bakery after the evening rush. She's twenty-six, she works forty hours a week, and she earns exactly the legal minimum. On the bus home she checks her balance two days before payday. It isn't a lot. But it isn't zero, and her rent, her health insurance, and her transit pass for the whole month are already paid.

Four thousand eight hundred miles away, outside Jackson, Tennessee, Tyler is closing out the register at a convenience store off the highway. He's also twenty-six, he also works forty hours a week, and he also earns exactly the legal minimum, seven dollars and twenty-five cents an hour, the same number it's been since before he was in middle school. He checks his balance in the parking lot, does the math against his car insurance and his half of the rent, and puts the phone face down. Same age, same hours, same job at the bottom of the pay scale. One of them ends the month with a little room to breathe. The other ends it in the hole, and he hasn't even been to a doctor yet.

I'm Justin, an American who's lived in Germany for over twelve years, and this channel is about the costs nobody puts on a postcard. Germany's minimum wage just went up to thirteen euros ninety an hour. America's federal minimum is still seven twenty-five. But an hourly wage is meaningless until you know what it buys, so I took real, current 2026 prices and rebuilt one full month of life around a minimum wage paycheck in each country, line by line, measured in the one currency both workers actually spend: hours of their own life. I haven't punched a minimum wage time clock in a long time, and I show all the math.

The strongest case for the other side

First, a fair caveat, because it matters. Almost nobody in America actually earns seven twenty-five an hour anymore. Thirty states plus Washington, D.C. have minimum wages above the federal floor (over seventeen dollars in Washington state, nearly eighteen in D.C.), and even in the twenty states that still use seven twenty-five, most employers pay more just to find workers. America also leans on tax credits and food assistance to top up low wages instead of raising the floor, so the paycheck alone undersells what a low earner receives. All true. But the federal minimum is still the legal floor in twenty states, and people really do live on it. Tyler isn't a hypothetical, just less common than he used to be, so we'll run his month honestly, help included.

The gross paycheck, and the deductions

Germany's statutory minimum wage, the gesetzlicher Mindestlohn, applies nationwide in every industry. At thirteen ninety an hour, forty hours a week, Jana grosses about two thousand four hundred and nine euros a month. Tyler, at seven twenty-five, grosses about twelve hundred fifty-seven dollars. More than double. And Germany's is reset every couple of years by an independent commission, the Mindestlohnkommission, already scheduled to rise to fourteen sixty in January 2027. America's federal minimum hasn't moved since July 2009, during which US prices rose well over forty percent.

Then come the deductions, and this is where Jana's paycheck shrinks fast. Her social insurance contributions, Sozialabgaben, take roughly twenty-two percent of gross, around five hundred twenty-five euros: pension 9.3%, health 7.3% plus half of her insurer's extra premium, unemployment 1.3%, and long-term care 2.4% (including a surcharge for adults without children). Then wage tax, Lohnsteuer, takes roughly another one hundred seventy euros. Her take-home lands around seventeen hundred fifteen euros. About seven hundred euros of her gross never reaches her account.

Tyler's deductions look gentle by comparison. Social Security and Medicare take 7.65%, about ninety-six dollars. And his federal income tax is zero, because his annual income of about fifteen thousand dollars falls under the sixteen-thousand-one-hundred-dollar standard deduction. Tennessee has no state income tax, so his take-home is roughly eleven hundred sixty dollars, and in spring he gets a small earned income tax credit back. That's the one place America is clearly more generous to the lowest earner. It's just that seventy-five percent of not-much is still not much.

Rent: the line that decides everything

Jana lives in Dortmund, where a small forty-five square meter apartment runs around four hundred thirty euros in base rent, the Kaltmiete, plus utilities and building costs, the Nebenkosten, of about a hundred fifty, for a total warm rent, the Warmmiete, of about five hundred eighty. That's roughly a third of her take-home, about forty-two hours of work. Before moving in, her landlord wanted a security deposit, the Kaution, of up to three months' base rent, close to thirteen hundred euros, ninety hours of work saved up before she got the keys.

Tyler's rent has a twist. A modest one-bedroom in a smaller Tennessee city lists around eight hundred fifty to a thousand dollars, but many landlords require tenants to earn three times the rent, which would cap him at about four hundred twenty dollars a month, an apartment that mostly doesn't exist. So he splits a two-bedroom with a roommate: his half is about five hundred dollars plus seventy-five for electricity (air conditioning runs most of a Tennessee summer day). That's close to half his take-home, nearly eighty hours of work, almost two weeks of his month gone before a single meal.

City matters just as much in Germany. The same bakery job in Munich, where asking rents top twenty-one euros per square meter, would put that same apartment well over nine hundred fifty euros base, more than eleven hundred warm, two-thirds of her take-home. The minimum wage is identical in Munich and Dortmund down to the cent. The rent is not. A minimum wage worker in Chemnitz, around six euros a square meter, lives a completely different life from one in Munich on the exact same paycheck.

Health, groceries, and getting to work

Jana's health insurance is already handled: that seven-plus percent out of her paycheck buys full coverage under statutory public health insurance, the gesetzliche Krankenversicherung, no deductible, with prescription copays of five to ten euros and an annual cap. Tyler's health insurance is the biggest surprise in this whole comparison, and we'll come back to it.

Groceries are where the hours-of-work lens bites. At Germany's discount supermarkets, a careful single-person budget is around two hundred fifty euros a month, about eighteen hours of Jana's work. A comparable US budget runs closer to three hundred fifty dollars, about forty-eight hours of Tyler's. Same cart, nearly three times the work. Getting to work splits them completely: Jana rides the nationwide transit pass, the Deutschlandticket, at sixty-three euros a month, about four and a half hours of work. Tyler has no bus, so he runs a paid-off used car: roughly a hundred dollars insurance, a hundred twenty gas, sixty to eighty for maintenance, call it three hundred a month, about forty-one hours of work, and that's with no car payment.

Time off, overtime, and the safety net

By law Jana gets at least four weeks of paid vacation, and if she's sick her employer keeps paying her full wage for up to six weeks, the Entgeltfortzahlung im Krankheitsfall. A minimum wage worker in Germany never has to choose between a fever and a paycheck. Tyler has no federal right to a single paid day off; every sick day costs him about fifty-eight dollars, so in practice he goes in sick. Tyler does have one real advantage: US law pays time-and-a-half past forty hours, while Germany has no general overtime premium. Jana's lever is a small side job, a Minijob, up to six hundred three euros a month free of income tax and contributions.

Both countries patch the gap at the bottom, just differently. Tyler likely qualifies for food assistance (SNAP), roughly a hundred to a hundred fifty dollars a month, plus his tax credit. Germany offers a housing allowance, Wohngeld, for low earners whose rent eats too much, worth little in Dortmund but very real for a minimum wage earner in Munich. America patches with food and a refund; Germany patches with housing money, mostly in expensive cities.

Retirement: who replaces more?

Here's a genuine surprise. Jana's pension contributions, matched by her employer, would after forty-five years of full-time minimum wage work add up to a pension of roughly a thousand euros a month at today's values (which is why Germany has a basic supplement, the Grundrente, for long low-wage careers). But America's Social Security formula is actually more generous to the lowest earners: it replaces about ninety percent of the first slice of average earnings, so a lifetime at Tyler's wage produces a benefit around eleven hundred dollars a month, roughly ninety percent of what he earns now. Germany pays its minimum wage worker more today. America, surprisingly, replaces more of a minimum wage income in retirement.

The coverage gap: Tyler's health insurance

Now the one we kept coming back to. Working full-time at seven twenty-five, Tyler earns about fifteen thousand eighty dollars a year. The 2026 federal poverty line for a single person is fifteen thousand six hundred fifty, so a full-time minimum wage job in America pays slightly below the poverty line. In the roughly ten states that never expanded Medicaid, including Tennessee, that creates a trap: adults without children generally can't get Medicaid no matter how little they earn, but Affordable Care Act subsidies only start at one hundred percent of the poverty line. Tyler earns too little to qualify for help buying insurance and lives in a state where he can't get Medicaid either. Researchers call it the coverage gap, and it means the most likely health insurance status for a full-time minimum wage worker in Tennessee is none at all. Jana, in Dortmund, has never had to think about any of this, because her coverage came out of her paycheck before it reached her account.

She ends the month with somewhere around five hundred fifty euros left: not wealth, but a cushion she can save. Her essentials cost about seventy-three hours of work out of the hundred seventy-three in her month.

What to actually do about it

  1. Compare any minimum wage against rent in that specific city, not the national number. In both countries, local rent is what decides whether the wage works or doesn't.

  2. If you're a low earner in Germany, check whether you qualify for the Wohngeld housing allowance, especially in an expensive city, and ask your employer about a subsidized job ticket. Both are commonly left unclaimed.

  3. If you're a low earner in the US, file a tax return even if you owe nothing, because that's the only way to collect the earned income tax credit, and check your state's Medicaid and SNAP rules before assuming you don't qualify.

  4. If you're planning a move to Germany on a lower salary, budget for net pay, not gross. Plan on losing around thirty percent to deductions, and save three months of base rent for the deposit before you start apartment hunting.

What a minimum wage actually buys

Jana will be back behind that counter Monday, and in January her wage rises again, to fourteen sixty. Tyler will be back behind his register too, at the same seven twenty-five it's been for seventeen years. Neither is getting rich. But one of them is working a month that adds up, and the other is working a month that doesn't. That's what a minimum wage actually buys, and it's exactly the kind of cost nobody puts on a postcard.
